Model assumptions

  • Units flow serially: Units In for a step is the previous step's Units Out. No parallel paths, no merges.
  • Rework Scrap is a subset of Reworked, not an additional loss. Leave it at 0 to assume every reworked unit is repaired successfully and passes on.
  • Defects counts non-conformances, which can exceed the number of defective units when a unit carries more than one. Left blank it falls back to Scrapped + Reworked, in which case DPU is a defective rate and e^(-DPU) adds no information beyond TPY.
  • Σ Step DPU sums rates measured on each step's own entering quantity, so it is not defects per starting unit. Both figures are shown on that card.
  • Every unit is inspected at every step; escapes and false accepts are not modelled.

Understanding Yield Metrics

A Lean Six Sigma guide to the metrics that reveal your true process capability

Y

Y - Traditional Yield

Also called Classic Yield or Final Yield

Traditional Yield measures the percentage of units that leave a step as good product. Reworked units count as good, because they did eventually pass. It is the simplest yield metric and the one most operations reports quote, but it has a critical blind spot: it says nothing about what it cost to get there.

Y = (Units In - Scrapped - Rework Scrap) / Units In
Successfully reworked units count as good - the hidden factory

If 100 units enter and 5 are scrapped but 20 require rework, Y reports 95%. The 20% rework burden is invisible, creating the so-called hidden factory that erodes margins.

A note on FTY. Some sources call this First Time Yield; others use FTY to mean first-time-right, which is what this tool calls TPY. The two conventions are genuinely in circulation, so always check which one a report means. What is never in dispute: First Pass Yield excludes rework, so FPY is a synonym for TPY, never for this metric.

T

TPY - Throughput Yield

Also called First Pass Yield (FPY)

TPY counts only units that got through the step right the first time. If a unit needed rework to pass, it was not right the first time, and TPY reflects that reality. This is the yield an MBB actually reasons with.

TPY = (Units In - Scrapped - Reworked) / Units In
Both scrap and rework reduce yield

Same example: TPY = (100 - 5 - 20) / 100 = 75%. A dramatically different picture than 95%, and far more honest about true process capability. When multiplied across steps, this gives the Rolled Throughput Yield.

R

RTY - Rolled Throughput Yield

The probability of a clean run end to end

RTY multiplies the TPY of every step to give the probability that a unit traverses the entire process defect-free, with no rework at any stage.

RTY = TPY1 x TPY2 x ... x TPYn
Multiplicative - small losses compound rapidly

Even with 95% TPY at each of 10 steps, RTY drops to about 60%. This compounding is why RTY is the most powerful metric for exposing systemic waste across a value stream.

Normalized Yield is its companion: Ynorm = RTY^(1/n) answers "what is a typical step doing?" and makes processes with different step counts comparable.

The Poisson Yield Formula: Y = e to the power of negative DPU

Using Euler's number to model defect probability

When we know the average Defects Per Unit (DPU) at a step, the Poisson distribution models the probability that a unit has zero defects:

DPU = Total Defects / Units In

TPY = e^(-DPU), where e ≈ 2.71828 (Euler's number)
The base of natural logarithms

The critical word is defects, not defectives. A defect is one non-conformance; a defective is a unit carrying at least one. One unit with three defects contributes 3 to the defect count and 1 to the defective count. That difference is the entire reason this method exists, and it is why this tool gives Defects its own input column rather than deriving it from the scrap and rework unit counts.

If you leave Defects blank, it falls back to Scrapped + Reworked — a count of defective units. In that case DPU is really the defective rate p, e^(-DPU) reduces to e^(-(1 - TPY)), and it will always read slightly higher than the unit-based TPY because e^(-p) ≥ 1 - p for every p. The column tells you nothing new until you record real defect counts. Enter them, and e^(-DPU) correctly falls below the unit-based TPY whenever units carry more than one defect.

For the overall process you can either multiply each step's Poisson yield or compute e^(-ΣDPU); both produce the same result, because e^(-a) × e^(-b) = e^(-(a+b)).

From DPMO to Sigma Level

Normalising for complexity, then converting to Z

Yield alone cannot compare a two-part bracket with a two-thousand-part assembly. DPMO fixes that by dividing defects not by units but by opportunities — the number of ways a single unit could fail. Set Opportunities / Unit in the control bar to match your operational definition, and keep that definition stable, because DPMO is only comparable against itself.

DPO = Total Defects / (Total Units Inspected x Opportunities per Unit)
DPMO = DPO x 1,000,000

Z = NORMSINV(1 - DPO)
Sigma Level = Z + 1.5 (when the shift is applied)
3.4 DPMO gives Z = 4.5, which becomes the familiar 6 sigma after the shift

The 1.5σ shift is a convention, not a measurement. It exists because a process centred today drifts over weeks and months, and empirical work at Motorola suggested long-term performance sits about 1.5 standard deviations worse than a short-term study predicts. The toggle is deliberate: Z from your data is long-term; adding 1.5 reports the short-term equivalent that industry benchmark tables assume. Quote one or the other, say which, and never compare a shifted figure to an unshifted one.

! Rework Considerations in the Formulas

How each metric treats rework is what fundamentally separates them and determines how honest they are about your process health:

  • Traditional Yield ignores rework entirely. A reworked unit that eventually passes is counted identically to one that passed perfectly the first time. This creates the dangerous hidden factory where rework costs, cycle time inflation, and resource consumption stay invisible to management.
  • TPY and RTY penalize rework. Any unit requiring rework is treated as a failure at that step. This exposes the hidden factory and gives a truthful measure of process capability for improvement prioritization.
  • The Poisson model counts every individual defect — but only if you feed it defect counts. If one unit has 3 defects found and corrected, it contributes 3 to Defects and 1 to Reworked. That is the highest-resolution view of process quality available, and it is why the Defects column is separate.
  • Rework that fails becomes scrap. The Rework Scrap column captures units that entered rework and never came back. Left at zero, the model assumes every repair succeeds, which is optimistic in most real processes and worth checking before you quote a number.
  • Rework inflates cycle time and cost without adding value. In Lean methodology, rework is pure waste (Muda). Even if a process shows 98% Traditional Yield, a low RTY reveals the true cost of poor quality as every rework loop consumes labor, materials, machine time, and floor space.

A process might show 95% Traditional Yield but only 60% RTY. That gap — reported directly as the Hidden Factory Gap above — represents resources consumed by rework, retest, and repair that never appear in simple yield numbers but directly erode margin, delivery performance, and customer satisfaction.
